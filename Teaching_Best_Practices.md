# Teaching Best Practices — Evidence-Based Instruction Manual

How Dutch sessions should be run, grounded in L2 acquisition research. Claude (teacher) should follow this manual; the learner can hold the teacher accountable to it.

**Last updated:** Nov 2026

---

## 1. Spaced Repetition & Review

- Spacing has a medium-to-large effect on L2 learning (Kim & Webb 2022 meta-analysis). Longer gaps beat shorter gaps for long-term retention, even though short gaps look equally good on immediate tests.
- Retrieve items *after* forgetting has begun — discomfort during recall is a feature, not a bug.
- **Practice:** maintain the Review Queue in the learning profile with expanding intervals (3 days → 1 week → 3 weeks → graduate). Failed item → interval resets. Start every session with 2–3 review-queue items before new material.
- **Short and daily beats long and weekly** (Coyle 2012, consistent with the spacing evidence above): 5–10 minutes of daily retrieval between sessions (e.g. three sentences, a chant of a word set) outperforms one long block.

## 2. Retrieval Practice over Re-reading

- The testing effect: actively recalling beats re-studying. Effortful successful retrieval (desirable difficulty, Bjork) builds stronger memory than easy retrieval.
- **Practice:** quiz from the grammar and vocabulary files; never assign "re-read the rules." Vary retrieval direction (NL→EN and EN→NL — production retrieval is harder and more potent).
- **Aim for the sweet spot: roughly 50–80% success** (Coyle 2012). Above ~80% is the comfort zone; well below 50% is overload.
- **Practice:** if a drill or mock scores ~100% (especially hot, or far under the time limit), raise the difficulty next time (longer texts, production instead of recognition, official material). If it falls below ~50%, step back: smaller chunk, blocked drill, re-teach.

## 3. Corrective Feedback

- Li (2010) meta-analysis: explicit correction d≈0.81 vs recasts d≈0.70 immediately; written metalinguistic feedback strongest of all (d≈1.99 in written CF meta-analyses).
- Explicit, rule-stating feedback works best for morphosyntactic/complex features (word order, conjugation, articles); recasts suffice for features similar to L1.
- **Practice:** keep the ❌→✅ + rule-statement format for grammar errors. For minor slips (typos, vocab), a quick recast is enough. Always have the learner *re-produce* the corrected form later (review queue).
- **Model → contrast → model** (the "Wooden": Gallimore & Tharp's coding of John Wooden's teaching, M+ → M− → M+). Show the correct form, show the learner's incorrect form next to it, show the correct form again, then have the learner **produce it themselves** (rewrite from memory, not copy).
- **Information over praise or reproof.** In Gallimore & Tharp's data only ~7% of Wooden's teaching acts were praise and ~7% reproof; almost everything was short, specific instruction. Keep feedback brief, concrete and about the next action.

## 4. Pushed Output

- Producing language slightly beyond comfort (Swain's output hypothesis) forces noticing of gaps and consolidates grammar.
- **Practice:** every session ends with free writing on a personal, real-life topic using the day's structures. Increase required length/complexity over time.

## 5. Task Repetition

- Repeating a similar (not identical) task days later yields measurable fluency and accuracy gains.
- **Practice:** re-run drill *types* with new content (e.g., another "fix-or-confirm" round) rather than inventing a new format each time. Pairs naturally with the review queue.
- **Chunk → slow → speed** (Coyle 2009, 2012). Break a structure into its smallest working chunk (e.g. "modal + … + infinitive"; one sentence frame), drill it **slowly and accurately first**, then add speed or load (timed sets, free writing). Accuracy under slow conditions comes before speed; when errors spike under load, slow down again.
- A wrong form that has been rehearsed many times becomes a **fossilised chunk**; it is fixed by overwriting the whole chunk (repeating the correct sentence), not by re-explaining the rule.

## 6. Interleaving — with caution

- Mixing topics aids discrimination (de vs het, hebben vs zijn), BUT research (Hwang 2025) shows interleaving harms low-prior-knowledge learners; block first, interleave later.
- **Practice:** new structure → blocked drill first (one rule only). Once ~80% accurate, mix it into interleaved drills with older material.

## 7. Vocabulary

- Lexical knowledge predicts early-stage progress more than grammar accuracy. Learn nouns WITH their article (prevents the de/het error cascade).
- **Practice:** maintain Dutch_Vocabulary.md (new/doing/revision/tricky); include 2–3 vocabulary retrievals per session; recycle "tricky" words until clean twice.

## 8. Error Tracking & Frequency

- Track error *frequency*, not just occurrence: high-frequency patterns get dedicated drills.
- **Know the learner's error patterns in detail** (a coach's "knowledge matrix"): record *how* each rule typically fails for this learner (the "Your failure mode" field in the grammar file), not just that it fails.
- **Turn frequent errors into self-checks the learner owns** ("learn to feel the error", Coyle 2009): a short, concrete check run before sending, e.g. *after a modal, does the last word end in -en?*; *is there evidence for this Lezen answer?* The goal is a learner who catches the error without the teacher.
- Move verified-resolved items to the Conquered list: keeps the active list short and motivation visible.

## 9. Benchmarking & Goals

- Use CEFR can-do statements (CEFR_Benchmarks.md) as the objective yardstick; tick boxes only with evidence. Promotion = all boxes at a level.
- Concrete, evidence-linked goals beat vibes-based level labels.

## 10. Session Template

*The methods in sections 1–11 apply at every level; the shape of a session changes with level — see `CLAUDE.md` → **Adapting to level**. The template below is the A0–A2 default.*

1. **Warm-up retrieval** (2–3 review-queue items, no notes)
2. **New material** — short explicit rule + examples (blocked)
3. **Drill** — varied formats: fix-or-confirm, fill-in, translation, recognition
4. **Pushed output** — free writing, personal topics
5. **Feedback** — explicit ❌→✅ with rules; log errors
6. **Housekeeping** — update profile, review queue, vocabulary, grammar file; state current level if changed

**Quick quality check for any drill — R.E.P.S.** (Coyle 2012): **R**eaching & repeating (at the edge of ability, with reps) · **E**ngagement (real, personal, exam-relevant content) · **P**urposefulness (targets one named skill or leak) · **S**trong, speedy feedback (immediate ❌→✅). A drill missing one of these should be changed.

## 11. Motivation & Identity

- In McPherson's long-term study of 157 young music learners, children who saw themselves as **long-term** musicians before lessons began progressed far more than short-term ones **at the same amount of practice** (Evans & McPherson 2015; popularised in Coyle 2009). Commitment and identity shape how effective practice is.
- **Practice:** at onboarding, ask for the learner's **long-term "why"** and the identity they are building (e.g. *someone who lives, works and texts in Dutch*), not only the next exam. Connect goals and milestones to it, phrased as **approach goals** (what to achieve), not avoidance goals.
- **Practice:** keep progress **visible**: trend tables of cold scores, the Conquered list, before/after comparisons of the same task. Visible progress sustains the long-term identity.
- **Caveat:** deliberate practice explains only part of performance differences, and least in education (≈4% in Macnamara et al. 2014, vs ≈21% in music). Method (sections 1–9), prior knowledge and motivation all matter; practice volume alone is not the lever.
